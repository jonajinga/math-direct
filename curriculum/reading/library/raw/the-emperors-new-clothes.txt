# The Emperor Who Loved Clothes

Many years ago there lived an emperor who cared for nothing but new clothes. He spent all his money on them and all his time admiring them. He did not care about his soldiers or about riding in the park. He only wanted to show off his latest outfit. He had a different coat for every hour of the day. When people asked for him, they were told he was in his dressing room.

The city where he lived was busy, and strangers arrived every day. One day two swindlers came to town. They said they were weavers who could make the most wonderful cloth. Not only were its colors and patterns beautiful. The cloth had a magic power as well. It was invisible to anyone who was foolish or unfit for his job.

"Those would be splendid clothes," thought the emperor. "If I wore them, I could tell which men are unfit for their jobs. I could tell the clever from the foolish at a glance." So he paid the two swindlers a large sum of money. He told them to begin their work at once.

V: emperor = a ruler who is greater than a king
V: swindler = a person who cheats others out of their money
V: invisible = not able to be seen

# The Empty Looms

The swindlers set up two looms and pretended to weave. But there was nothing on the looms at all. They asked for the finest silk and the purest gold thread. All of it went straight into their own bags.

After a few days the emperor grew curious about the cloth. But he felt a little uneasy about going himself. What if he could not see it? So he sent his honest old minister to look instead. "He is clever, and nobody is better at his job," the emperor thought.

The old minister went into the room where the swindlers sat weaving. He opened his eyes very wide. "Mercy on us, I cannot see a thing," he thought. But he did not say so out loud.

The swindlers begged him to come closer and admire the pattern. They pointed at the empty looms and described the colors. The poor minister stared and stared, but there was nothing to see. He wondered, "Can I be foolish, or unfit for my job? I must never let anyone find out."

"It is charming, quite charming," he said at last. "Such a lovely pattern, and such rich colors." The swindlers smiled and asked for more silk and gold. The minister reported to the emperor that the cloth was beyond compare.

# The Emperor Sees for Himself

Soon the emperor sent another trusted official to see how the work went. The same thing happened to him. He looked and looked, but he saw only empty looms. "I am not foolish," he told himself. "So it must be my job that I am unfit for. It would never do to let anyone know." So he praised the cloth as highly as the minister had.

The whole city was talking about the magnificent cloth. At last the emperor decided to see it for himself. He took a crowd of his finest gentlemen along with him.

The swindlers were weaving away with all their might. There was not a thread on the looms. The two officials said, "Is it not magnificent, Your Majesty? See the pattern and the colors."

The emperor thought, "What is this? I cannot see anything at all. Am I a fool, or am I unfit to be emperor?"

"Oh, it is very pretty," he said out loud. "It has my highest approval, and I am very pleased." All of his gentlemen stared too, and they saw no more than he did. But they all cried out, "Beautiful, beautiful," along with the emperor. They told him to wear the new clothes in the grand procession. The emperor gave the swindlers a medal and the title of Royal Weavers.

V: official = a person with an important job for the ruler
V: magnificent = very grand and beautiful
V: procession = a line of people walking along for a special event

# The Procession

The night before the procession, the swindlers sat up with sixteen candles burning. They cut the air with great scissors and sewed with needles without thread. At last they announced, "The emperor's new clothes are ready."

The emperor came with his gentlemen, and the swindlers held up their arms. "Here are the trousers, and here is the coat," they said. "You will feel as if you had nothing on, and that is their beauty."

The emperor took off his clothes, and the swindlers pretended to dress him. He turned this way and that in front of the mirror. "How well they suit me," he said.

So the emperor walked in the procession under his fine canopy. Everyone in the streets said how beautiful the new clothes were. Nobody would admit that they saw nothing, for fear of seeming foolish.

"But he has nothing on," said a little child. "Dear me, listen to the child," said his father. Then the people whispered what the child had said to one another. "He has nothing on," they all cried at last.

The emperor shivered, for he knew they were right. But he thought, "I must go through with the procession now." So he walked on prouder than ever. And his servants walked behind him, holding up a train that was not there.

V: canopy = a cloth roof held up on poles
V: admit = to say that something is true
V: train = the long part of a robe that drags behind

Q: What is a canopy? | A cloth roof held up on poles over someone. | vocabulary
Q: What does the story teach? | People pretend to see what others see because they fear looking foolish. | idea
