# Our Nearest Neighbor

The moon is a great ball of gray rock. It travels around the earth, and
it is our nearest neighbor in space. The moon is about one fourth as
wide as the earth.

The moon looks bright, but it makes no light of its own. The sun shines
on it, just as the sun shines on the earth. The moon's rock reflects
some of that sunlight back to us. That is the light we call moonlight.
The sun always lights one half of the moon. The other half is dark, as
it is night there.

The moon is far away, about two hundred forty thousand miles. Light from
the moon takes more than a second to reach us. A car driving day and
night at highway speed would take about five months. The moon goes once
around the earth in about twenty seven days. It also spins around once
in that same time. So the same side of the moon always faces the earth.
No one saw the far side until a spacecraft flew around it in 1959.

The moon pulls on the earth, too. Its pull tugs at the seas and makes
the tides. Twice each day the sea rises up the shore and falls back.
That rise and fall is mostly the work of the moon.

V: neighbor = a person or thing that is close by
V: reflect = to bounce light back
V: tides = the rise and fall of the sea each day
Q: How long does the moon take to go around the earth? | The moon goes around the earth in about twenty seven days. | literal
Q: Why do we always see the same side of the moon? | It spins once in the same time it goes around the earth. | inference
Q: What are tides? | Tides are the rise and fall of the sea each day. | vocabulary
Q: What is this reading mostly about? | The moon is a rock that circles the earth and reflects sunlight. | idea

# The Changing Moon

The moon seems to change its shape from night to night. Some nights it
is a thin curve, and some nights a full circle. These shapes are called
the phases of the moon.

The moon does not really change its shape at all. The sun always lights
half of it. What changes is how much of that lit half we can see. As the
moon circles the earth, our view of it changes.

At the new moon, the moon lies between the sun and the earth. Its lit
side faces away, so we cannot see it. A few nights later a thin crescent
shows in the evening sky. After about a week, half of the moon's face is
lit. A week after that, the moon is full. Now the earth lies between the
sun and the moon. The whole lit side of the moon faces us. Then the moon
shrinks night by night until it is new again.

One whole round of phases takes about twenty nine and a half days. The
word month comes from the word moon. Long ago, people counted the months
by watching the moon.

Many people think the earth's shadow makes the phases, but it does not.
The earth's shadow falls on the moon only during an eclipse.

V: phases = the shapes the moon seems to have each month
V: crescent = a thin curved shape, like a fingernail
V: eclipse = a time when the earth's shadow covers the moon
Q: What is the moon called when we cannot see it at all? | It is called the new moon, with its lit side facing away. | literal
Q: Why can we see more of the moon each night after the new moon? | As the moon moves around the earth, more of its lit half faces us. | inference
Q: What is a crescent? | A crescent is a thin curved shape, like a fingernail. | vocabulary
Q: What is this reading mostly about? | The phases happen because we see different parts of the moon's lit half. | idea

# On the Moon

The moon has no air around it. With no air, there is no wind and no
weather. Sound cannot travel there, so the moon is silent.

The ground is covered in fine gray dust and broken rock. Everywhere
there are craters, round holes with raised rims. Rocks from space made
them when they crashed into the moon. On the earth, most space rocks
burn up in the air. On the moon there is no air to burn them up.

The moon is a place of great heat and great cold. In the day the ground
is hotter than boiling water. At night it is far colder than any place
on the earth. The moon's pull is only one sixth as strong as the
earth's.

On July 20, 1969, two people walked on the moon for the first time. They
were Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, from the United States. A third
man, Michael Collins, circled the moon in their ship. Armstrong stepped
down first onto the dust. He called it one small step for a man, one
giant leap for mankind. Their footprints are still there, for no wind
will ever blow them away. Between 1969 and 1972, twelve people walked on
the moon.

V: craters = round holes in the ground with raised rims
V: giant = very large
V: mankind = all the people in the world
Q: Who were the first two people to walk on the moon? | Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the moon in 1969. | literal
Q: Why does the moon have so many more craters than the earth? | The moon has no air to burn up rocks from space. | inference
Q: What does mankind mean? | Mankind means all the people in the world. | vocabulary
Q: What is this reading mostly about? | The moon is a dusty, airless place, and people have walked on it. | idea
