# Where a River Begins

Every river begins with water that falls from the sky. Rain and snow
fall on hills and high mountains. Snow lies on the peaks all winter and
melts in spring. Water always runs downhill, so it trickles down the
slopes.

Some of the water soaks into the ground. It moves slowly through cracks
in the rock. In some places it comes back out as a spring. A spring is a
place where water flows up out of the ground. Many rivers begin at a
spring.

Tiny trickles join together and make a small stream. Streams join other
streams and grow wider and deeper. In time the streams make a river. A
stream that flows into a river is called a tributary. A big river may
have hundreds of tributaries. The place where a river begins is called
its source. All the land that drains into a river is its basin.

The Mississippi River begins as a small stream in Minnesota. At its
source a child can wade across it. Then it flows south for more than two
thousand miles. Water from thirty one states ends up in it. In some
places it grows to almost a mile wide.

V: tributary = a stream that flows into a bigger river
V: source = the place where a river begins
V: wade = to walk through water that is not deep
Q: What is a stream that flows into a river called? | A stream that flows into a river is called a tributary. | literal
Q: Why does a river grow wider as it flows along? | More and more streams join it and add their water. | inference
Q: What does wade mean? | To wade means to walk through water that is not deep. | vocabulary
Q: What is this reading mostly about? | Rivers begin with rain and snow and grow as streams join them. | idea

# How a River Shapes the Land

As a river flows, it picks up sand, soil, and small stones. It carries
them along and wears away its bed and banks. Over thousands of years it
can cut a deep valley. The Colorado River cut the Grand Canyon, about a
mile deep.

In the mountains a river runs fast and straight. On flat land it slows
down and begins to wander. It swings from side to side in wide loops
called meanders. On the outside of a bend the water runs fastest. There
it wears the bank away. On the inside the water runs slowly and drops
sand. Sometimes the river cuts straight across a bend. The old loop is
left behind as a curved lake.

When heavy rain comes, a river may rise over its banks. The flood
spreads soil across the flat land beside the river. When the water goes
down, the soil stays. This makes some of the richest farmland in the
world.

At last the river reaches a lake or the sea. This end of a river is
called its mouth. Here the water slows down and drops the soil it
carries. The soil piles up and builds new land, often shaped like a fan.
This land is called a delta. The Nile and the Mississippi both end in
great deltas.

V: bank = the ground along the side of a river
V: meanders = wide loops in a slow river
V: delta = new land a river builds at its mouth
Q: What did the Colorado River cut into the land? | The Colorado River cut the Grand Canyon, about a mile deep. | literal
Q: Why does the river build new land at its mouth? | The water slows there and drops the soil it was carrying. | inference
Q: What is a delta? | A delta is new land a river builds at its mouth. | vocabulary
Q: What is this reading mostly about? | A flowing river wears away land in some places and builds it in others. | idea

# Rivers and People

People have always lived near rivers. A river gives water to drink and
water for crops. Many of the great cities of the world grew up on
rivers. London sits on the Thames, and Paris on the Seine.

Rivers are roads for boats, too. A boat can carry a heavy load that no
cart could. On the Mississippi, long barges carry grain and coal. Dams
across rivers hold back the water to make lakes. The water that rushes
through a dam turns machines that make electricity. Rivers also give us
fish, and places to swim and paddle.

A river belongs to everyone along it, so it must be kept clean.
Everything that falls on the land in a river's basin can wash into it.
Oil from the street and trash from the park wash down the drains. Waste
from farms and factories can run in, too. Dirty water harms the fish,
the birds, and the people downstream.

Years ago, one river in Ohio was so dirty that it caught fire. After
that, laws were passed to stop factories from dumping waste. Today that
river is much cleaner, and the fish have come back. Children can help
keep rivers clean, too. Pick up litter near a stream, and never pour
anything down a street drain.

V: barges = long flat boats that carry heavy loads
V: electricity = the power that runs lights and machines
V: downstream = farther along the way the river flows
